# The 60/60 study system

In my first year at Howest, studying MTS3-CS, I passed everything on the first attempt. 60 out of 60 ECTS. No retakes. GPA 16.40 out of 20.

People hear that and assume I am either a genius or I never slept. I am not a genius, and I did sleep (poorly, but that is a lifestyle choice, not a study strategy). What I had was a system. It is not complicated, it is not glamorous, and it works for the most boring reason possible: it is built around how learning actually happens instead of how studying feels.

Here it is, all of it.

## The unsexy truth first

There is no hack. Anyone selling you a "study 10x faster" method is selling you something. Learning technical material is slow because your brain needs to build the mental models, and mental models are built by struggling with the material, not by reading about it.

Everything in my system is designed to maximize one thing: **time spent actively wrestling with the material** versus time spent passively consuming it. Reading slides feels productive. It is mostly not. Doing the lab, getting stuck, figuring out why: that feels terrible and is where all the learning happens.

Once you accept that, the system designs itself.

## Labs before theory

This is the core of the whole thing, and it is the opposite of what most students do. Most students read the theory first, then attempt the lab, then wonder why the lab feels impossible.

I do the lab first. Cold. No preparation.

Here is why: when you read theory first, everything looks obvious. "Oh sure, a subnet mask, I understand." Then the lab asks you to actually subnet something and your brain files a missing-person report. Reading creates the illusion of understanding. Doing destroys it, which is exactly what you want, because now you know precisely what you do not understand.

So the sequence is:

1. **Attempt the lab with zero preparation.** Get stuck. Get stuck is the goal. Note exactly where you get stuck and what confuses you.
2. **Now read the theory**, with your list of confusions open. Theory stops being abstract words when you are reading it to answer a specific question you already have. "Why did my route not work" is a much better reading prompt than "chapter 4".
3. **Redo the lab.** This time it clicks, because the theory has somewhere to land.

This takes longer per lab than the read-then-do approach. It also means I almost never have to re-study the material later, because I actually learned it the first time. Slow is smooth, smooth is fast.

## Notes like you teach

I take notes in markdown, one file per course, and I write them as if I am explaining the material to someone else. Not "subnet mask: 255.255.255.0" but "a subnet mask tells the machine which part of the address is the network and which part is the host, here is how to read it, here is where I messed it up in lab 3".

Two rules for the notes:

**Rule 1: no copying slides.** If the sentence in my notes could have been copy-pasted from the slides, I delete it and rewrite it from memory. Copying is transcription, not learning. Rewriting from memory forces retrieval, and retrieval is the mechanism that builds memory. This is the single highest-leverage study habit I know.

**Rule 2: every concept gets a why.** Not just what a thing is, but why it exists and what breaks without it. "DNS exists because humans cannot remember IP addresses" is worth more than a page of record types. In exams, the why is what lets you reason through questions you have never seen. The what only lets you answer questions you have memorized.

My notes are also where my mistakes live. Every error I made in every lab, what caused it, what fixed it. Before an exam, I read my mistake list. It is the highest-density review material I own, because those are precisely the things I am most likely to get wrong again.

## The weekly rhythm

I do not do marathon study sessions. I do not have a color-coded timetable that I follow for three days and abandon. My week is simple:

- **After each lecture or lab session:** 30 to 60 minutes, same day, rewriting that day's notes from memory and redoing anything that felt shaky. Same-day review is disproportionately effective because the material is still warm. Skip this and you will pay for it threefold later.
- **Weekend:** one longer session per course that needs it, focused on the week's labs. Redo the hard ones from scratch, without looking at my previous solution. If I cannot redo it, I did not learn it.
- **No zero weeks.** Even in slow weeks, I touch every course at least briefly. Knowledge decays. A 20-minute review prevents a 3-hour relearning session later.

Total: less than what most students think. The difference is not hours, it is that every hour is active. An hour of redoing a lab from scratch beats four hours of re-reading slides, and it is not close.

## Exam prep: the last two weeks

Two weeks before exams, I switch modes. No new material. Only consolidation.

**Week minus two:** I go through every course's notes and mark each topic green (could teach it), yellow (understand it but shaky), or red (danger). Then I spend the week turning reds into yellows and yellows into greens, using the lab-first method on the weak spots. Past exam questions enter here: I do them under timed conditions, because knowing the material and performing under time pressure are different skills.

**Week minus one:** full practice exams, timed, no notes. Then I review every mistake and add it to the mistake list. I also redo the hardest labs one final time. By the last few days, I am mostly reviewing my own notes and mistake lists, not learning anything new. If you are learning new material the night before the exam, the system failed three weeks ago.

**The night before:** I stop early. Seriously. Cramming until 4am the night before an exam is how you turn knowledge into fog. (Do I always follow this rule? No. Has it ever helped when I broke it? Also no. Learn from my failures, not just my system.)

## Exam day

Boring advice, but boring works: read the whole exam first, answer the easy questions first to bank points and calm down, then attack the hard ones. If a question confuses you, write down what you know about the topic; partial credit is real and "I blanked" is not a strategy.

Time management is the actual exam skill. I allocate time per question based on points and move on when the time is up, even if it hurts. An unfinished 10-point question and an unstarted 10-point question both score zero, but the second one never even got a chance.

## The sleep schedule disclaimer

I have to address this because people see "16.40 GPA" and picture me studying 14 hours a day on perfect sleep. My sleep schedule is, by all accounts, terrible. I am often up at hours that concern my friends.

Here is the honest truth: I succeeded despite my sleep schedule, not because of it. Everything I know about cognitive science says I would have learned faster and retained more with proper sleep. The system worked anyway because the active-learning core is that strong. But do not romanticize the 3am sessions. They are a bug, not a feature. If you can run this system on eight hours of sleep, you will outperform me. I am slightly annoyed about that, but it is true.

## Why first attempt matters (to me)

Belgian universities give you a second exam session in August. I treated the first session as the only session. Not because retakes are shameful (they are not), but because of simple math: if you plan for August, you study for August. If January is the only option, you study for January.

This is psychological, not moral. A real deadline with no safety net focuses effort in a way that "I can always retake" never will. I gave myself no backup plan, and then I worked like I had no backup plan.

## The system in one paragraph

Do the lab before reading the theory. Take notes by rewriting from memory, as if teaching someone. Record every mistake. Review same-day, redo labs from scratch weekly, and spend the last two weeks consolidating instead of learning. Treat the first exam session as the only one. Sleep properly (do as I say, not as I do).

That is it. No apps, no subscriptions, no 5am club. Just active learning, consistently, for a year. 60 out of 60.

The system is not the hard part. Showing up every week is the hard part. But that was always going to be true.
